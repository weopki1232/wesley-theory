Molecular Genetics · Act I

The Central Dogma

How a cell copies its genome with near-perfect fidelity — the machinery of DNA replication, built one enzyme at a time.

DNA → RNA → Protein

A genome is information. To divide, a cell must copy 3 billion letters — and barely miss.

Replication is the first arrow of the dogma: DNA → DNA.

We'll meet every player, then watch them work at the fork.

Meet the molecule

B-form DNA — an antiparallel ladder

10.4 bp / turn rise 3.4 Å / bp Ø 20 Å · right-handed

Two backbones run antiparallel — one 5′→3′, its partner 3′→5′.

The rungs are base pairs: each base reads its complement across the helix.

A:T is held by 2 hydrogen bonds — weaker, easier to melt.

G:C is held by 3 hydrogen bonds — stronger, higher melting temperature.

Geometry is fixed: ~10.4 bp per helical turn, 3.4 Å rise per base.

Step 1 · open the helix

Unwinding at the replication fork

The fork is a Y: ahead lies closed duplex, behind it two open strands.

Topoisomerase runs ahead, releasing the twist that builds up as the fork moves.

Helicase sits at the junction and pries the two strands apart.

SSB beads cling to the bare strands so they stay open and unkinked.

Step 2 · lay the starter

Primase writes an RNA primer

DNA polymerase has one weakness: it can extend, but it cannot start.

Primase lays a short RNA primer — a 3′-OH handle the builder can grab.

Step 3 · build · the two strands diverge

Leading is smooth — lagging is in pieces

Both new strands grow only 5′→3′ — but the fork opens in one direction.

Leading strand: Pol III chases the fork in one continuous sweep.

Lagging strand: built backwards in Okazaki fragments, each re-primed.

The trombone loop lets both polymerases move as one unit.

Step 4 · close the seams

Pol I erases the primers, ligase seals

Each fragment still begins with an RNA primer — that has to go.

Pol I chews out the RNA and back-fills DNA; ligase seals the nicks.

Why it barely ever fails

Three multiplicative layers of fidelity

1 · base selection
10⁻⁵
polymerase picks the right base
2 · 3′→5′ proofreading
10⁻⁷
exonuclease backs up & corrects
3 · mismatch repair
10⁻¹⁰
MMR sweeps the survivors

A genome can't tolerate many errors — so accuracy is built in layers.

Selectivity alone gives ~1 error in 100,000. Good, not enough.

Proofreading multiplies it to ~10⁻⁷ — the polymerase corrects itself live.

Mismatch repair finishes the job: ~1 error in 10 billion bases.
